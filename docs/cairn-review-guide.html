For reviewers

Where the claims are, how to check each one yourself, what this project has already got wrong, and where we most expect to be wrong still.

For release 0.14.0, 29 October 2026 github.com/cairnchain/cairn Every command below was run against that release before publishing

How this was made

Thank you for looking. This page is for someone who knows the field and has an afternoon.

Cairn's code, documents and tests were written by an AI model (Claude), directed by one person who does not program. Every audit so far was done by AI models too: many of them, adversarial and enumerative, and they found real defects, some of them serious, which are listed below with the pull request that fixed each. No human outside the project has reviewed any of it before you. Please weigh everything here accordingly, and assume nothing has been checked by a person.

1

The design in five sentences

Every node holds a hot set of notes capped by consensus rule at 131 072 notes, about 68 MB. Older notes live in an append-only Merkle forest (the Utreexo construction) carried as at most 64 roots, and are spent with an inclusion proof the spender supplies, checked against the roots as they stand now. A note that just fell out of the hot set stays spendable with no proof for a grace window of 64 blocks, at most 8 192 notes. Every place a transfer takes in the hot set destroys a fixed place price of 6 000 pebbles, so pushing notes out costs whoever does it. A newcomer joins from a FlyClient-style sample of the header chain and a ledger handed over by a peer, weighed rather than replayed.

The paper is the whitepaper; the protocol, byte for byte, is the specification; what is defended and what is not is the threat model.

2

The three questions we most want answered

  1. The sampling bound (whitepaper, "Sampled verification"). 4 096 draws, measured to hold against a forger up to at least 42.96% of the world's work (published as 40%), with a guarantee of about 633 blocks of depth. Measured, not proved, over the placement families in crates/cairn-ledger/examples/adversarial_placement.rs and crates/cairn-ledger/examples/searching_for_a_break.rs; the draw itself is crates/cairn-ledger/src/sampling.rs. The first version was wrong by a factor of 2122, and the paper says how. Is there a strategy outside those families, or an input the prover controls that we missed?
  2. A forged burial from an old fork point. The difficulty is ASERT anchored at the first block, so the time elapsed since an old fork point is real and free to declare, and no difficulty rule prices a rewrite from far back. What prices it is the weighing and the refusal of a tip more than 32 times below the hardest header of its run, which puts a tip at no less than 2^18.9 hashes on testnet-8 (crates/cairn-ledger/tests/the_price_of_a_seed.rs). Naive forgeries from fork points one hour, twenty-eight hours and ten days old are refused (crates/cairn-ledger/tests/audit_a_burial_from_three_fork_ages.rs). An optimally built one has not been built. A handed-over ledger on its own does accept a forged burial, by design: the weighing is the gate (crates/cairn-ledger/tests/audit_forged_join.rs says why).
  3. The thesis against Christ and Bonneau (whitepaper, "The limit that applies, and where Cairn falls"). Cairn takes the proof-update side of their dilemma on purpose. Does a hot set capped by consensus rule change who bears the proof-update rate, as the paper claims, or only how it is described?

If you have more time: the place price against a flood of the hot set, the tie band in the fork choice, and ASERT anchored at the first block with a bound of four per block.

Where this project has been wrong

Listed because a reviewer should know the kind of mistake this process makes. Each was found in-house, by an AI audit, a lab test or the live test network, and fixed with a test that fails without the fix. These are the ones rated critical or high, one line each; findings that were the same defect share a line. The audit reports themselves are not published.

Before pull requests were used, in the design phase:

  • The sampling bound, first version: 512 draws and 45.7%, derived for a uniform draw; the real draw left a forger 2-5.8 instead of 2-128.
  • Borrowed weight: the sampling was sound about how much work a chain carried and said nothing about whether the tip stood at its end.
  • Proofs were accepted against any of the last 32 states; removing a note through an old path folded nothing, and the note could be spent again. Every node agreed, so nothing forked.

Since, by pull request:

  • The number of halvings the draw spreads over came from a height the prover states, which let a forger buy halvings with invented work (#1).
  • A fresh node on a network whose first block is pinned never joined by handover (#244).
  • A forged body held first for a side-branch block kept a node off the heavier branch (#244).
  • The specification did not say how a signature is verified, where Ed25519 verifiers differ (#246).
  • A peer's frame, transfer or block request was decoded or read before its allowance was charged, so a greeted peer bought seconds of processor per message, and one join answer was built per asker (#242).
  • Timestamps the rules accepted from a minority could make the chain run at half speed (#237).
  • A path let go of on a running ledger was not put back by a reorganisation, so the node refused a valid block (#237).
  • The documents priced seed grinding at the tip's own work, when a tip at the floor cost one nonce (#237, then #255, which holds a tip to the hardest header of its run).
  • A block from a second un-updated peer got that peer refused as misbehaving (#243).
  • A node's inbound slots could be held for good by strangers that behaved, and every per-host rule keyed on the exact address, so one IPv6 machine passed them all (#243).
  • The start-up replay deleted valid blocks on a refusal that was about the reader, and an archivist lost every leaf at its first restart (#236).
  • A node that missed one block from a peer it took as its equal stopped following the best chain (#240).
  • A heavier branch of full blocks parting less than a batch below the tip was never taken (#251).
  • A wallet's node told a stranger which key it followed (#249).
  • Wallet accounting after a reorganisation: notes kept that the losing branch paid, movements below a trimmed log shown as taken back, undone payments left in the account (#248).
  • A payment send handed over was forgotten by the next command and could be sent twice, and send built payments from a chain it had not finished reading (#238).
  • A payment a block carried could be called unpaid, with "send it again", which could pay the payee twice; and a restored account could be rewound by its own node catching up (#254).
  • The wallet called its key file the only copy and named nothing about history.dat, so a restore from the key lost every note that had fallen cold, and an unreadable account was written over (#233).
  • A wallet counted two silent sockets as two peers and said it had caught up (#256).
  • The block log took any refused read for its end and cut every later block, and the explorer showed a balance of nought past the money ceiling (#230).
  • The explorer answered "no such transaction" for one it had located, and showed a stopped node under a sentence promising recovery (#234).
  • The documents said an unspent note shows no key, while the wallet paid its change back to the address whose key a spend had just published (#265).
  • The difficulty was a moving average until a stranger mined 80 blocks in two minutes on testnet-7 and the network nearly stopped for 33 hours (#278).
  • About 4 096 junk side blocks claiming difficulty 1 made a node drop an honest heavier branch, and the side store's bounds broke after a failed switch (#281).
  • 62 reused addresses kept a newcomer off the honest chain for ever, and a node killed outright lost its anchors (#282).
  • The gate that held a newcomer for the choice of chain read the height the peer wrote into its own handshake, so one peer could put a newcomer on a false chain; critical (#296).
  • A heavier branch handed over past 32 MiB of side blocks lost its top block as each one landed (#295).
  • A stranger with one address more than a list held kept a newcomer off the honest chain, and a wallet that opened none of its first four archivist dials stopped looking (#294, #296).
  • The wallet told a person left on an abandoned branch to delete pending.dat, its only record of payments in flight (#292).
  • A miner whose every connection lost the duplicate tie never mined, live on testnet-8 (#298).
  • A node never asked for a branch parting more than 72 blocks below its tip, so a fork 73 to 1 024 deep was legal and impossible to read; live on testnet-8 (#300).
  • A peer the choice asked to read was followed past the depth a node undoes with its claimed work never checked, so one visitor could put a newcomer on a worthless chain for good; critical (#306).
  • A restarted miner's wait for a heavier chain was spent by the first peer it reached, whatever that peer claimed (#305).

An audit of everything that changed since 14 October was started on 29 October 2026; what it finds will be added here.

What is known and open

These are stated in the documents and not fixed:

  • A stall a stranger can still cause. The time a newcomer gives one peer's read of its chain is bounded, at 543 s on testnet-8 (#310), but the bound is per connection: a stranger holding N connections can hold a newcomer up to N times that before the honest chain is read. A stall, not a capture.
  • Only the Linux releases can be rebuilt byte for byte on another machine (deploy/rebuild.sh, #309). The macOS and Windows archives are built twice on GitHub and compared, but their linkers and system libraries come with the machine; deploy/README.md says which match.
  • R4, the optimal forged burial from an old fork point, the second question above. A lab attempt to build the cheapest one was not finished, and the question is left to outside review.
  • A fake archivist that answers for a chain and never sends the blocks costs a recovery a minute per batch when it is the only peer that could serve them.

The open questions lists what the project has decided not to decide yet: privacy, post-quantum signatures, who archivists are and who pays them, the difficulty variants set aside, and fee estimation. The whitepaper's "Limitations" section is the list of what the design does not do.
